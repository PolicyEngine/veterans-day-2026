## A retiree with a second career

The retiree in the first couple served from 2001 to 2020, and the spouse has no income.

{{chart:map_working_45}}

Oregon and DC tax all of the pay, so the couple owes {{tax:working_45:OR}} more in Oregon and {{tax:working_45:DC}} more in DC. Oregon subtracts the share of a federal pension that comes from service before October 1, 1991 ([ORS 316.680(1)(e)](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors316.html)), and this retiree began serving in 2001. DC hasn't excluded any military retired pay since 2015 ([D.C. Code § 47-1803.02(a)(2)(N)(i)](https://code.dccouncil.gov/us/dc/council/code/sections/47-1803.02)).

Four states exempt up to a fixed amount of the pay for this couple. Delaware exempts up to $12,500 for filers under 60 and raises that to $25,000 by 2029 ([30 Del. C. § 1106(b)(3)](https://delcode.delaware.gov/title30/c011/sc02/index.html)). Maryland exempts the first $12,500 for filers under 55 ([Tax–General § 10-207(q)](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/mgawebsite/Laws/StatuteText?article=gtg&section=10-207&enactments=false)), and Colorado exempts up to $15,000 for filers under 55 through 2028 ([C.R.S. 39-22-104(4)(y)](https://content.leg.colorado.gov/sites/default/files/images/olls/crs2024-title-39.pdf#page=397)). California exempts up to $20,000 from 2025 through 2029 for joint filers with federal adjusted gross income (AGI) up to $250,000 ([FTB Publication 1032](https://ftb.ca.gov/forms/2025/2025-1032-publication.pdf#page=3)). On the rest of the pay, the couple owes {{tax:working_45:DE}} in Delaware, {{tax:working_45:MD}} in Maryland, {{tax:working_45:CO}} in Colorado and {{tax:working_45:CA}} in California.

Montana exempts half of the pay, up to the couple's earnings in Montana, for five consecutive years ([MCA 15-30-2120](https://archive.legmt.gov/bills/mca/title_0150/chapter_0300/part_0210/section_0200/0150-0300-0210-0200.html), subsections (3)(n), (8) and (9)). For this couple Montana exempts $15,000, and the couple owes {{tax:working_45:MT}} on the other half.

Utah, South Carolina and Maine exempt all of the pay but count it as income for a credit or deduction that they reduce as income rises. Utah gives a credit that offsets its tax on the pay ([2025 TC-40 instructions, p. 22](https://files.tax.utah.gov/tax/forms/2025/tc-40inst.pdf#page=23)). But Utah also cuts its taxpayer tax credit by 1.3% of income above a base amount ([p. 9](https://files.tax.utah.gov/tax/forms/2025/tc-40inst.pdf#page=10)), so the couple loses {{tax:working_45:UT}} of that credit.

South Carolina deducts all military retired pay ([Revenue Ruling 22-11](https://dor.sc.gov/sites/dor/files/policies/RR22-11.pdf), on S.C. Code § 12-6-1171), but it shrinks its $30,000 Income Adjusted Deduction for married couples once their federal AGI exceeds $80,000 ([Act 110 of 2026](https://www.scstatehouse.gov/sess126_2025-2026/bills/4216.htm), section 3). With the pay, the couple's federal AGI reaches {{usd:working_45:SC:federal_agi_military}}, so South Carolina cuts the deduction by $2,720 and the couple owes {{tax:working_45:SC}} more.

Maine deducts the pay too ([36 M.R.S. § 5122(2)(M-2)](https://legislature.maine.gov/statutes/36/title36sec5122.html)), but it counts the pay as income for its sales tax fairness credit ([§ 5213-A](https://legislature.maine.gov/statutes/36/title36sec5213-A.html)), so the couple loses a {{tax:working_45:ME}} credit.

In Alabama and Missouri the couple owes less state income tax with the pay than without it. Both states exempt the pay ([2025 Form 40 booklet, p. 14](https://www.revenue.alabama.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/25f40bk.pdf#page=14); [RSMo 143.121.3(12)](https://revisor.mo.gov/main/OneSection.aspx?section=143.121)) and let filers deduct federal income tax, which rises by {{fedchange:working_45}} with the pay. Alabama allows the full deduction ([Form 40 booklet, p. 8](https://www.revenue.alabama.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/25f40bk.pdf#page=8)) and Missouri 15% at this income ([RSMo 143.171](https://revisor.mo.gov/main/OneSection.aspx?section=143.171)), so the couple owes {{tax:working_45:AL}} less in Alabama and {{tax:working_45:MO}} less in Missouri.

## A retired couple

The retiree in the second couple served from 1977 to 1996.

{{chart:bar_retired_67}}

DC and Montana tax all of this couple's pay, and Delaware taxes all but $12,500, the most it exempts for filers 60 and older. Montana caps its exemption at a couple's earnings in Montana, and this couple has none. The couple owes {{tax:retired_67:DC}} more in DC, {{tax:retired_67:DE}} in Delaware and {{tax:retired_67:MT}} in Montana.

Hawaii, Maine, Indiana and Arizona exempt all of the pay ([Form N-11 instructions, p. 13](https://files.hawaii.gov/tax/forms/2025/n11ins.pdf#page=13); [§ 5122](https://legislature.maine.gov/statutes/36/title36sec5122.html); [IT-40 booklet, p. 22](https://forms.in.gov/Download.aspx?id=16915#page=22); [A.R.S. § 43-1022(26)](https://www.azleg.gov/ars/43/01022.htm)), but each gives a credit to households with income below a limit. With the pay, {{usd:retired_67:HI:taxable_social_security_military}} of the couple's Social Security becomes taxable and their federal AGI rises from $0 to {{usd:retired_67:HI:federal_agi_military}}, so the couple loses:

- Hawaii's food/excise tax credit, {{tax:retired_67:HI}}, which Hawaii gives married couples with federal AGI under $60,000 ([2025 Form N-311](https://files.hawaii.gov/tax/forms/2025/n311_i.pdf));
- Maine's sales tax fairness credit, {{tax:retired_67:ME}} ([§ 5213-A](https://legislature.maine.gov/statutes/36/title36sec5213-A.html));
- Indiana's unified tax credit for the elderly, {{tax:retired_67:IN}}, which Indiana gives to filers with income under $10,000 ([IT-40 booklet, p. 30](https://forms.in.gov/Download.aspx?id=16915#page=30));
- Arizona's credit for increased excise taxes, {{tax:retired_67:AZ}}, which Arizona gives married couples with federal AGI of $25,000 or less ([A.R.S. § 43-1072.01](https://www.azleg.gov/ars/43/01072-01.htm)).

Idaho charges {{tax:retired_67:ID}} to every person it requires to file an income tax return ([Idaho Code § 63-3082](https://legislature.idaho.gov/statutesrules/idstat/title63/t63ch30/sect63-3082/)), and with the pay this couple has to file.

## How we calculated these

We calculated each couple's 2026 state income tax in every state twice with [PolicyEngine-US](https://github.com/PolicyEngine/policyengine-us) [[TODO: release version]], once with the military retired pay and once without it. Both couples file jointly, claim no dependents and live in the state all year, and the retiree served 20 years and has no service-connected disability. North Carolina exempts the pay of retirees who served at least 20 years or retired for medical reasons ([G.S. 105-153.5(b)(5a)](https://www.ncleg.gov/EnactedLegislation/Statutes/HTML/BySection/Chapter_105/GS_105-153.5.html)). Kentucky exempts the share of a federal pension that comes from service before 1998 ([2025 Schedule P](https://revenue.ky.gov/Forms/Schedule%20P%20%282025%29.pdf)), and Oregon the share from service before October 1991. All of the 67-year-old's service predates 1998, and 177 of its 240 months predate October 1991. In Montana, we assume the retiree meets the exemption's residency and five-year conditions. We count state credits, including refundable ones, in state income tax and leave out local income taxes.

PolicyEngine's microdata record VA payments but not veteran status or military retired pay, so we don't estimate how many retirees each state's rules reach. We publish the code and full results, including a wider grid of ages, pensions and wages, at [github.com/PolicyEngine/veterans-day-2026](https://github.com/PolicyEngine/veterans-day-2026).
